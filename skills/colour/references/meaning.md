# Meaning: emotion and culture

What published studies found about colour and feeling, with the scope of each finding, and how meaning is built in a site. Each claim carries its kind: **Standard**, **Empirical finding** or **Heuristic**.

A finding informs a choice and can be cited in the palette entry with its scope ("in this study…"). It never promises what a visitor will feel. No study here tested websites: they used colour fields, colour patches or colour words.

## Lightness, chroma and hue together
- **Empirical finding**: Wilms & Oberfeld (2018) showed 62 observers fields of coloured LED light, varying hue, saturation and brightness, and measured felt arousal and valence. More saturated and brighter colours raised arousal. Valence was highest for saturated, bright colours and also depended on hue. Several effects interacted: the emotional effect of a colour depends on all three dimensions, not on hue alone.
- Scope: one lab, one population, large uniform light fields, no content. A website's colours sit next to each other, under text and images, on many screens.
- **Heuristic**: settling lightness and chroma before hue is a working order this finding supports. It is not a law: hue had effects of its own.

## Shared associations, with variation
- **Empirical finding**: Jonauskaite et al. (2020) asked 4,598 participants in 30 nations, in 22 languages, to associate 20 emotion concepts with 12 colour **terms**. Patterns were largely shared (average similarity between nations r = .88). Nation still predicted the associations beyond that pattern, and nations closer in language or geography were more alike.
- Scope: colour words, not displayed colours; an online questionnaire; associations, not felt emotion. A word like "red" covers many very different colours.

## Liking follows what a colour evokes
- **Empirical finding**: Palmer & Schloss (2010) proposed the ecological valence theory: people tend to like a colour to the degree that they like the things they associate with it, such as clear sky for many blues or spoiled food for some dark yellows.
- Scope: average preferences for isolated colour patches. Individual preferences vary and shift with experience.
- **Heuristic**: what a colour evokes in this audience, in this context, is a better guide than a supposed universal meaning.

## Colour emotion scales
- **Empirical finding**: Ou, Luo, Woodcock & Wright (2004), in three parts. In Part I, 31 observers (14 British, 17 Chinese) rated 20 colours; the ratings reduced to colour activity, weight and heat. In Part II, the emotion rating of a two-colour combination was close to the mean of its two colours, but preference was not. Part III modelled preference for single colours.
- Scope: a small sample from two cultures, patches under controlled conditions, pairs at equal areas.
- **The equations are not included.** Never compute, recall or guess their coefficients, and never present an emotion score for a palette. Use these findings in words only.

## Culture: a question to ask
There is no table of what a colour means by country. When the audience is specific, ask whether a colour carries a strong association for it: a religious or political colour, a mourning colour, a rival's colour. If nobody knows, say so in the palette entry rather than presenting a guess as a fact.

## Meaning is built in the site
- **Heuristic**: a colour comes to mean what the site keeps pairing it with. If the accent is on every action and nowhere else, it soon reads as "you can act here". If it also decorates headings, it stops meaning anything.
- **Standard**: whatever a colour comes to mean, it never carries information alone (WCAG 2.2, SC 1.4.1).
- There is no symbolism table in this skill. "Blue means trust" ignores lightness, chroma, context, culture and the site's own pairings.
