# The laws of UX

Laws are evidence for a rule, not rules themselves. Most were observed in a lab, on one task, decades ago, and then simplified on their way into design. Read a law here before leaning on it. Grouped by the decision they serve.

The laws belong to their original authors. Laws of UX (Jon Yablonski, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0) is the reference that gathers them; none of its text is reused, and the descriptions below are ours.

| Law | What it really says | Kind | Source | Misuse | Serves |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mental Model | People act on a simplified inner picture of how a system works (Craik, 1943) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Designing from the team's picture of the product instead of the user's | `path-8`, `words-5` |
| Jakob's Law | People expect a site to work like the sites they already use (Nielsen) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Copying a competitor's flaws, or refusing any new pattern | `path-6` |
| Paradox of the Active User | People skip the manual and start using the software at once (Rosson and Carroll, 1987) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Relying on a tour or a help page to explain the interface | `path-11` |
| Pareto Principle | A small share of causes often produces most of the effects (after Pareto) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Treating 80/20 as a measured ratio, or as a reason to neglect the rest | `path-10` |
| Tesler's Law | Some complexity cannot be removed: either the system or the user carries it (Tesler) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Hiding complexity instead of absorbing it, or designing for an idealised rational user | `path-12` |
| Hick's Law | Decision time grows with the number of equally likely choices, logarithmically (Hick, 1952; Hyman) | empirical finding | Hick 1952, Laws of UX | Hiding options to lower a number. It measures choosing among alternatives; a well-sorted long list stays quick to use | `path-15` |
| Choice Overload | Too many comparable options can overwhelm and stall a choice (Toffler, 1970) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Removing the options people came to compare | `path-15` |
| Cognitive Load | Understanding and using an interface takes mental effort, and that effort is limited (Sweller, 1988) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Removing useful information in the name of simplicity | `path-15`, `thread-4` |
| Goal-Gradient Effect | Effort rises as a goal gets closer (Hull, 1932, in rats; human evidence is thinner) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Faking progress to push people on | `path-13` |
| Zeigarnik Effect | Unfinished tasks were remembered better than finished ones (Zeigarnik, 1920s) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Inventing unfinished tasks to nag people back | `path-13` |
| Working Memory | A small system briefly holds and handles what we are thinking about; its capacity varies with people and context | empirical finding | Laws of UX, Cowan 2001 | Quoting one fixed capacity as a design limit | `path-14`, `words-15` |
| Miller's Law | About seven items, plus or minus two, can be held in short-term memory (Miller, 1956); later work puts it nearer four chunks (Cowan, 2001) | empirical finding | Miller 1956, Cowan 2001, Laws of UX | Capping a menu at seven items. What stays visible does not have to be remembered | `words-15` |
| Chunking | Grouping pieces into meaningful wholes lets us hold more of them (Miller, 1956) | empirical finding | Miller 1956, Laws of UX | Turning it into a fixed number of items per group | `words-15` |
| Selective Attention | People attend to what serves their current goal and miss much of the rest (Cherry, 1953; Broadbent, 1958) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Assuming that what is on the page gets seen | `thread-4` |
| Serial Position Effect | The first and last items of a series are remembered best (Ebbinghaus) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Applying it to lists people search rather than read in order | `thread-8` |
| Occam's Razor | Among explanations that predict equally well, prefer the one with the fewest assumptions (William of Ockham) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Removing what does matter because "simpler is better" | `thread-3` |
| Flow | Full absorption in an activity when challenge matches skill (Csíkszentmihályi, 1975) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Trying to immerse people who only want to finish and leave | `behaviour-3` |
| Doherty Threshold | Productivity rose when system and user answered each other within about 400 ms (Doherty and Thadani, IBM, 1982) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Treating 400 ms as a universal limit: it comes from computer work of that era | `behaviour-4` |
| Postel's Law | Be liberal in what you accept and strict in what you send (Postel, from the TCP specification) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Silently accepting an ambiguous value instead of asking | `behaviour-9` |
| Fitts's Law | The time to reach a target grows with its distance and shrinks with its size (Fitts, 1954) | empirical finding | Fitts 1954, Laws of UX | "Bigger is always better": the gain is logarithmic, so enlarging a target that is already large adds little | `behaviour-14`, `behaviour-15` |
| Peak-End Rule | An experience is judged mostly by its peak and its end (Kahneman et al., 1993, on discomfort) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Neglecting the middle of a journey because "only the end counts" | `states-5` |
| Law of Proximity | Things close together are seen as related (Gestalt psychology) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Deciding it in the plan: grouping on screen is a visual decision | layout |
| Law of Similarity | Things that look alike are seen as a group (Gestalt psychology) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Making unrelated things look alike | layout |
| Law of Common Region | Things inside a shared boundary are seen as a group (Gestalt psychology) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Boxing everything, which is how pages become sealed blocks | layout |
| Law of Uniform Connectedness | Things visually connected are seen as more related (Gestalt psychology) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Connecting elements that have no real relation | layout |
| Law of Prägnanz | Ambiguous shapes are read in their simplest form (Wertheimer, Gestalt psychology) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Using it to justify removing information | layout |
| Von Restorff Effect | The one item that differs from similar ones is remembered best (von Restorff, 1933) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Emphasising many things at once, so that nothing stands out | layout |
| Aesthetic-Usability Effect | People rate attractive interfaces as easier to use (Kurosu and Kashimura, 1995) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Taking good looks as proof of usability: they hide problems in testing | not used: it is why the critic reads structure before visuals |
| Cognitive Bias | Mental shortcuts produce systematic errors of judgment (Tversky and Kahneman, 1970s) | empirical finding | Laws of UX | Exploiting biases with false urgency or pre-ticked consent | not used: too broad for a rule, and the shortest way to dark patterns |
| Parkinson's Law | Work expands to fill the time available (Parkinson, a humorous essay, 1955) | heuristic | Laws of UX | Citing an essay as evidence about users | not used: it concerns how long a task takes, not an interface decision |
